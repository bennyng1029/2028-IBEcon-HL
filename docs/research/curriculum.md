# IB Diploma Economics HL: curriculum and assessment research

Research date: 14 November 2026. Scope: May 2028 preparation, especially Units 1–2. This is a curriculum inventory and authoring guide, not an IB publication or claim of IB endorsement.

The nine cross-course concepts are scarcity, choice, efficiency, equity, economic well-being, sustainability, change, interdependence and intervention. The guide treats content as common SL/HL except when specifically identified as HL; detailed distinctions matter more than merely tagging an entire chapter. Recommended HL teaching time is 10, 70, 75 and 65 hours for Units 1–4, plus 20 for IA ([IB HL brief](https://www.ibo.org/globalassets/new-structure/programmes/dp/pdfs/hl-economics-en.pdf), pp. 1–2).

## Unit 1 — introduction (common SL and HL)

- **1.1 What is economics?** Economics as social science; micro/macro; nine concepts; four factors of production; scarcity, sustainability, choice, opportunity cost and free goods; what/how much, how and for whom; market, planned and mixed systems and government intervention. Draw and explain PPCs including scarcity, opportunity costs, constant versus increasing opportunity cost, productive efficiency, unemployment, actual growth and growth in capacity. Explain and diagram the circular flow including households, firms, government, finance, foreign sector, injections and leakages. (Guide pp. 22–23.)
- **1.2 How economists approach the world:** positive versus normative statements; logic, hypotheses, models, ceteris paribus, evidence and refutation; value judgments and equity versus equality. Trace ideas at the specified introductory depth: Smith and laissez-faire; classical utility, marginal thinking, Say's law and Marxist critique; Keynesian and monetarist/new classical changes; behavioural thinking and economy–society–environment relationships/circular economy. (Guide pp. 23–24.)

## Unit 2 — microeconomics

Each entry distinguishes common content from additional HL requirements. Diagram and calculation expectations below form an authoring checklist, not a substitute for the guide's AO-specific depth table.

- **2.1 Demand** (pp. 25–26): law, demand curve, individual versus market demand; income, preferences, expected prices, substitutes/complements and number of consumers; movement along versus shift. **HL:** income and substitution effects and diminishing marginal utility as assumptions underlying demand. Draw and explain movements and shifts.
- **2.2 Supply** (p. 26): law, individual versus market supply, curve, movement versus shift; factor costs, joint/competitive supply, indirect taxes/subsidies, expected prices, technology and number of firms. **HL:** diminishing marginal returns and increasing marginal costs underlying supply. Draw and explain movements and shifts.
- **2.3 Competitive equilibrium** (pp. 26–27): equilibrium and adjustments following changes in supply or demand, shortage and surplus; price mechanism's signalling, incentive and rationing functions; consumer, producer and community surplus; allocative efficiency and MB = MC. Draw changes and welfare areas. **HL:** calculate consumer and producer surplus from diagrams.
- **2.4 Critique of maximizing behaviour** (p. 27): **HL only**, including rationality, utility maximization and perfect information; bounded rationality, self-control and selfishness, imperfect information, rules of thumb, anchoring, framing and availability; choice architecture, default/restricted/mandated choices and nudges; profit and alternative objectives including CSR, market share, satisficing and growth.
- **2.5 Elasticity of demand** (pp. 27–28): PED and YED formulas, signs, interpretations, degrees, relevant diagrams, determinants, changes in total revenue and practical use of PED; normal/inferior, necessity/luxury and Engel curves; calculate elasticity and missing changes from data. **HL:** changing PED along a straight-line demand curve, primary commodity versus manufactured-product PED, and YED implications for firms and sectoral change.
- **2.6 Elasticity of supply** (p. 28): PES formula, interpretation, diagrams, time, factor mobility, capacity, storage and cost changes; calculate PES or missing price/quantity changes. **HL:** why primary commodity supply tends to be less elastic than manufactured-product supply.
- **2.7 Government in microeconomics** (pp. 29–30): reasons for intervention; price ceilings/floors, indirect taxes, subsidies, direct provision, legislation/regulation and effects on stakeholders; draw market consequences, evaluate policy. **HL:** consumer nudges and calculate effects of controls, taxes and subsidies.
- **2.8 Externalities and common resources** (pp. 30–31): MSB = MSC, efficiency and welfare loss; positive/negative production and consumption externalities, merit/demerit goods; common pool resources, rivalry/non-excludability, overuse and sustainability. Diagram all four externality types and responses. Consider indirect/Pigouvian and carbon taxes, subsidies, regulation, education, tradable permits, international agreements, collective self-governance and public provision; evaluate measurement, effectiveness, stakeholders and international cooperation/enforcement. **HL:** calculate welfare loss from diagrams.
- **2.9 Public goods** (p. 31): non-rivalry, non-excludability, free riders, and evaluation of government provision versus contracting out. Common SL/HL.
- **2.10 Asymmetric information** (p. 31): **HL only**; adverse selection, moral hazard, legislation/regulation, public information, private signalling and screening.
- **2.11 Market power** (pp. 31–33): **HL only**; characteristics of perfect competition, monopoly, oligopoly and monopolistic competition; profit maximization using TR−TC and MR=MC, normal/abnormal profit and loss; calculate profit, MC, MR, AC and AR. Price takers/makers, short- and long-run outcomes, allocative efficiency, natural monopoly and monopoly welfare loss; oligopoly collusion, incentives, price/non-price competition, concentration ratios and simple payoff matrices; monopolistic competition and product variety. Diagram firm outcomes and relevant comparisons; evaluate economies of scale, innovation, prices, choice and policy responses such as regulation, ownership and fines.
- **2.12 Market inability to achieve equity** (p. 33): **HL only**; how free-market operations may generate unequal income and wealth distribution; use circular-flow reasoning and diagram.

Do not label all calculations HL: PED, YED and PES calculations are common, whereas specified surplus, controls and externality calculations are HL. Do not treat all behavioural economics or market structures as common SL content. Exclude unlisted extensions from required study unless a current guide establishes them; label any enrichment explicitly optional.

## Assessment and original practice

The [IB HL brief](https://www.ibo.org/globalassets/new-structure/programmes/dp/pdfs/hl-economics-en.pdf), p. 2, specifies external assessment 80%: Paper 1 extended response, 1 h 15 min, 20%; Paper 2 data response, 1 h 45 min, 30%; Paper 3 policy paper, 1 h 45 min, 30%. Internal assessment is 20%. The October 2022 guide, pp. 58 and 61–62, states Paper 2 is the same for SL and HL and excludes HL extension material; use HL-only material for Papers 1 and 3 as specified. Assessment objectives include knowledge, application/analysis, synthesis/evaluation, and written, diagrammatic and quantitative skills. The [IB examiner instructions for 2025](https://ibpublishing.ibo.org/exinst/apps/exinst/index.html?chapter=2&doc=EX_instructions_2025_e&part=10) describe P1 choosing one of three questions with 10- and 15-mark parts; P2 choosing one of two seven-part questions; and P3 policy recommendations using evidence, analysis and evaluation. Session-specific guidance may change; recheck when May 2028 instructions are released.

The guide, printed pp. 69–70, and [IB examiner instructions](https://ibpublishing.ibo.org/exinst/apps/exinst/index.html?chapter=2&doc=EX_instructions_2025_e&part=10) describe three commentaries, at most 800 words each, based on different units other than Unit 1 and different published news sources, using key concepts. Check the current guide for the full rubric and article-age rules.

**Evidence boundary:** the official IB [sample-paper page](https://ibo.org/programmes/diploma-programme/assessment-and-exams/sample-exam-papers/) does not list economics at the time checked. The IB says in its [subject guide access information](https://www.ibo.org/globalassets/new-structure/university-admission/pdfs/subject-guides/history-guide-en-2028.pdf), p. 1, that schools can use its Programme Resource Centre and that past papers/markschemes and Questionbank can be purchased via Titlewave; this general access statement is not evidence that a particular economics paper is publicly free. The initial inventory did not inspect a paper. Subsequent [source validation](source-validation.md) inspected a first-assessment-2022 specimen on a third-party host whose authorization is unverified. Exercises can be described as **syllabus- and specimen-format-informed**, but not as based on a representative survey of past examinations. Authorized school resources may permit a later gap analysis, subject to their terms.

Author original examples, data, questions, diagrams, worked explanations and scoring guidance from the syllabus and public examiner instructions; do not copy, translate, closely paraphrase or redistribute exam prompts, extracts, markschemes, graphics or passages. For each exercise attach topic, common/HL tag, objective, command word, intended marks and rationale. Progress from low-stakes concept checks to diagrams/calculations, then contextual explanation and stakeholder-aware evaluation; include defensible alternative answers. Distinguish house-created illustrative marks from official IB markschemes and explain uncertainty. Translate original educational content with consistent bilingual economic terminology, preserving axes, units, signs and underlying meaning; review each language against the same tagged source objectives.
